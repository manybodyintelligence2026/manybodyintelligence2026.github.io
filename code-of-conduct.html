FAQ Code of conduct

Code of conduct

When you confirm your place, we will ask you to confirm that you have read this page, and whether you want to be a co-author on the event paper.

Two kinds of paper can come out of the day. The event paper is a short paper the organisers write about the hackathon itself. A project paper is a research paper that a team may write later from its own results.

Working together

Be kind and patient with each other, whatever your career stage and however much you have used these tools. Many people will be trying them properly for the first time. Make sure everyone in your team gets to contribute, and in the peer review, criticise the work, not the people.

The university's rules on equal treatment and harassment apply as they do at work. If something goes wrong, talk to any of the organisers.

Using AI tools

Follow the university's guidance on AI tools. Don't put unpublished data from collaborators, anything covered by an agreement with an outside partner, or anything else confidential into an AI tool.

Check what the AI gives you. A result counts once someone on the team has checked it, and your write-up should say how.

Logs and data

Each team keeps a simple log in its repository: what you asked the AI to do, which model you used, what came back, what you checked, and what didn't work. Sharing full AI transcripts is optional. Team repositories are private.

We use the logs for the write-ups, the peer review and the event paper, and we anonymise them before quoting anything. We only collect the personal data we need to run the event, and our surveys are anonymous. Afterwards, notes, logs and code are archived in a division repository, or on Zenodo if they are part of the event paper.

The event paper

Everyone who takes part can be a co-author, and can change their mind until the paper is submitted. The organisers write the paper and are the corresponding authors. Everyone else is listed alphabetically, with a short statement of what they did, using the CRediT roles.

Each team's project gets its own section, describing what the team tried. It will not give details of any result that the team may want to publish later in a project paper.

Project papers

The result belongs to the team. If you carry on with it into a project paper, everyone who worked on it on the day is offered co-authorship, or an acknowledgement if they don't want to continue.

Being in someone's group does not make them an author. The usual rules apply, as in the Swedish Research Council's guide to good research practice: authors are the people who made a substantial contribution. A supervisor who joins the work later and contributes becomes an author in the normal way.

Talk to your supervisor before carrying on with a project. It takes time away from your thesis or grant-funded work, and the funding has to be acknowledged properly. PhD students should add it to their individual study plan.

Ideas you bring

Only bring an idea you are free to share. Anything that is part of your group's plans or an existing grant needs your PI's agreement first. Ideas pitched on the day will be heard by everyone in the room.

Senior staff

Professors and other senior staff are very welcome on teams. We ask that a PI doesn't join a team made up mainly of their own students and postdocs, so that people get to work with someone new. You can also take part without joining a team, for example by coming to the talks at the end of the day.

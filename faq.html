FAQ Code of conduct

FAQ

Signing up

Do I need a team?

We encourage you to talk to people in the division and form a team of 3–5, ideally mixing people from different groups. If that doesn't work out, sign up on your own and we will put you in a team.

Do I need a project idea?

No. After the deadline we will ask team leaders for a short idea, about 100 words. It should be something nobody on the team has worked on, and roughly the size of a Master's project.

Who can take part?

Anyone in the Materials Theory division: Master's students, PhD students, postdocs, researchers and professors.

Can I come for part of the day?

We know most people will have teaching, meetings or other commitments at some point during the day, so it's fine to step away for an hour or two. We do hope you can be there for most of the day, 09:00–17:00, since your team will be counting on you. If you are not on a team, you are welcome to come and listen to the talks at the end.

How much time does it take besides the day itself?

About an hour for a short online session the week before, to check that everyone's tools work. Team leaders also write the short project idea.

AI tools

I've hardly used AI tools. Is this for me?

Yes. Part of the point is to learn from each other, and the online session beforehand will get you set up. Skeptics are welcome too. Checking what the AI produces is a big part of the day.

Which tools do I need?

Most of the division already has Claude through their research group, and our material is written for Claude and Claude Code. If you don't have access, tell us and we will sort something out.

What should I bring?

Your laptop and its charger.

Can I use my own code, or our group's cluster?

Existing codes and methods are fine, but if the code or the computing resources belong to your group or to someone else, ask the appropriate people for permission first. Most projects should run on a laptop. If you think yours needs a cluster, let us know as well.

Is there anything I shouldn't put into an AI tool?

Yes: unpublished data from collaborators, anything covered by an agreement with an outside partner, and anything else confidential.

Credit and results

Two kinds of paper can come out of the day. The event paper is a short paper the organisers write about the hackathon itself. A project paper is a research paper that a team may write later from its own results.

Will I be a co-author on the event paper?

If you want to be. You can change your mind any time before it is submitted.

Who owns what my team comes up with?

The team. If you turn it into a project paper later, everyone who worked on it on the day is offered co-authorship.

Does my supervisor automatically become a co-author on a project paper?

No. The usual rules apply: a supervisor becomes an author if they contribute to the work. Do talk to your supervisor before you carry on with a project after the day.

Will the event paper give away our results?

No. The event paper describes how the day went and what each team tried, but it will not give details of any result that a team may want to publish later in a project paper.

Is any of this assessed?

No. There are a few small prizes for fun, and that's it.

On the day

What if my team doesn't get anywhere?

That's fine, and it's still useful to know. A clear account of what didn't work is a perfectly good outcome.

Is there food?

Yes, fika and pizza.
